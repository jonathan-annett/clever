A plain-language guide to measuring sound with clever

No prior experience with Smaart, REW or any other analyzer needed. Each tool is explained by the question it answers, then how to read it.

01What clever does

clever turns a laptop, tablet or phone plus a measurement microphone into an audio analyzer. It runs in Chrome or Edge with nothing to install. It answers questions like:

  • Which frequencies are loud in this room right now?
  • How loud is it, by the standards a venue or council uses?
  • Is something about to feed back, and which part of the spectrum keeps leading?
  • Is my stream or broadcast feed staying at the level I set?
  • What is the PA doing to the signal I send it, and did my EQ change fix it?
  • Are the subs and mains arriving at the same time?

What you need

  • A measurement microphone. clever has built-in support for the miniDSP UMIK-1 and the Dayton iMM-6C, which plug straight into USB. Any mic through an audio interface works too. Vocal mics such as an SM58 aren't flat enough to measure with.
  • Chrome or Edge. Other browsers lack some of the audio features clever needs.
  • The mic's calibration file (optional but recommended). See Your microphone.

02Two kinds of measurement

Every tool in clever falls into one of two groups. Knowing which you're using is most of the battle.

Single-channel: what does it sound like here?

One microphone, and clever reports what it hears. This is the RTA, the spectrograph and the SPL meter. These tools work on anything: a band playing, a playlist, pink noise, crowd noise. Think of them as very precise ears.

Their limit is that they show the music and the system together. If the RTA shows a bump at 200 Hz, you can't tell whether the room or PA is causing it, or whether the song simply has a lot of bass there.

Dual-channel: what is the system doing to my signal?

Two signals: the reference, which is what you sent into the system, and the measurement, which is what the mic picked up after it came out of the speakers and travelled through the room. clever compares the two, so the content of the signal cancels out and only the system's effect remains. This is the transfer function, shown as magnitude, phase and coherence.

Because the music cancels out, a transfer function works during a show with live programme material, not only with test noise. That's the main reason professionals use it.

03First five minutes

This gets you a working RTA and sound level meter.

  1. Plug in the measurement mic, then open clever in Chrome or Edge.
  2. Press Start. The browser asks for microphone permission; allow it. If your mic is a UMIK-1 or iMM-6C, clever usually selects it automatically. Otherwise choose it under Inputs › Measurement in the settings panel (the ☰ button on a phone).
  3. Watch the MEAS meter in the top bar move when you talk or clap. If it stays still, the wrong input is selected.
  4. The top plot is now a live RTA, and the number at the top right is the sound level in dBA.
  5. Load your mic's calibration file (next section) so those readings are accurate.
Tip

On a phone, clever opens with only the top plot showing. Tap ALL PANES in the top bar to see the transfer function plots as well.

04Your microphone

Why calibration files matter

No microphone is perfectly flat. A measurement mic comes with a small text file listing how far its response deviates at each frequency, made for that individual mic and tied to its serial number. clever subtracts those deviations so you see the room, not the mic. Without it, readings above about 10 kHz in particular can be several dB off.

Getting the file

  • miniDSP UMIK-1: open Microphone, choose miniDSP UMIK-1, and type the serial from the mic body, for example 700-1234. Pick 0° if you'll point the mic at the speakers, or 90° if you'll point it at the ceiling (common when measuring speakers all around you). Then use the link that appears to download the file.
  • Dayton iMM-6C: choose Dayton iMM-6C and follow the link to Dayton's calibration tool, where you enter the serial from the packaging.
  • Anything else: any text file of frequency and dB pairs works.

Load the file with Load cal file… or by dragging it onto the page. clever keeps it as a mic profile in that browser, so you only do this once per device. If you own several mics, each gets its own profile and you switch with Active mic. A UMIK-1's 0° and 90° files are separate profiles, labelled with their angle: use 0° with the mic pointed at the speaker, 90° with it pointed at the ceiling. Saved profiles lists them all, with a Delete button for any you no longer need.

Making the SPL reading accurate

The calibration file fixes the shape of the response. The absolute level, meaning how many dB SPL a given signal is, depends on the mic's sensitivity. The UMIK-1 file includes a sensitivity figure, so its readings start close to correct. The iMM-6C file doesn't, so its readings start as a rough guess.

The proper fix is an acoustic calibrator: a small device that fits over the mic and produces a known level, usually 94 or 114 dB SPL at 1 kHz. Fit it, press Start, enter the calibrator's level under SPL meter › Calibrator, and press Set offset from calibrator. The offset is saved to that mic's profile.

05RTA: which frequencies are loud right now?

RTA stands for real-time analyzer. It splits the sound into frequency bands and shows how loud each one is, updated many times a second.

How to read it

  • Left to right is frequency, from 20 Hz (deep sub bass) to 20 kHz (the top of human hearing). The scale is logarithmic: each step from 100 to 1k to 10k gets the same width, because that's how we hear pitch.
  • Height is level, in dBFS (relative to the loudest signal the input can take, so 0 is the top and numbers are negative) or in dB SPL if you switch Units.
  • Bars are bands. 1/3 oct is the classic view: 31 bands, each a third of an octave wide. Narrower bands such as 1/12 or 1/24 show more detail. Line draws a fine continuous curve.
  • The amber marks above the bars are peak hold: the highest recent level in each band, slowly falling.
  • Response sets how jumpy the display is. Fast follows the music; Slow averages over about a second and is easier to read.
Frame of reference

Pink noise has equal energy in every octave, so on a fractional-octave RTA it looks roughly flat. Play pink noise through a system and the RTA shows the system's overall tonal balance at the mic. Music never looks flat; that's normal.

Good uses

  • Spotting one band that sticks out far above its neighbours and won't go away: often a resonance, or feedback starting.
  • Checking the noise floor of a quiet room (air conditioning hum, buzz from a ground loop).
  • A quick sanity check of tonal balance with a familiar track.

06Spectrograph: what has been happening over time?

The spectrograph is an RTA with a memory. Frequency still runs left to right, but each new moment is drawn as a thin coloured line at the top and the history scrolls downward. Colour shows level: dark is quiet, through purple and orange, to pale yellow for the loudest.

Music makes a busy, shifting pattern. Problems leave straight vertical stripes: one frequency that stays loud while everything around it changes. That stripe is the classic early sign of feedback or a strong room resonance, and it is far easier to see here than on a jumping RTA.

Set Spec floor and Ceiling to bracket the levels you care about. If everything is dark, lower both; if it's all yellow, raise them.

07SPL meter: how loud is it?

The large number at the top right is the sound pressure level, the same measurement a handheld sound level meter gives. Three settings change what it means.

SettingOptionsWhat it means
WeightingA, C, ZA mimics how the ear hears at moderate levels and largely ignores deep bass. It's the standard for noise limits and hearing safety. C includes most of the bass and is used for low-frequency limits. Z is flat, with no weighting.
TimeFast, SlowHow quickly the number reacts. Fast (1/8 s) follows the music; Slow (1 s) is steadier.
Leqreset buttonThe equivalent continuous level: the average energy since you last reset it. Venue limits are usually written as Leq over a period such as 15 minutes, because a few loud hits matter less than sustained level.

Max is the highest reading since the last reset. Press Reset Leq / Max at the start of a set.

Labels on the readout tell you what you're looking at: dBA means A-weighted, dBC C-weighted. An unlabelled "100 dB" means little, so always quote the weighting.

08Levels and Bands: is it staying consistent?

Levels: watching a feed drift

The Levels tab keeps the last 10 minutes of level and shows the last 60 seconds by default. Zoom out to 3 or 10 minutes with the 60 s button, the mouse wheel, or − and =. It draws the level with a reference line and a large readout of how far the level has moved from it. Use it to keep a stream feed, a broadcast send or a presenter at a steady level over a long session.

  1. Choose what to watch with Mic in ▸: the measurement mic for the level in the room, or Ref in for a feed patched into your interface's reference channel, such as a desk or stream send.
  2. Once the level sounds right, press Set ref (or Enter). It takes the average of the last 10 seconds; change that under Levels › Ref from. You can also click or drag on the graph to place the reference exactly.
  3. Watch the readout. It shows the current level and the difference from the reference: green within 1 dB, amber within 3 dB, red beyond that. The 60 s avg line underneath is steadier, and is the one to watch for slow drift through pauses in speech or music.
  4. At the 60 s view, two boxes show the recent range: red for the last 5 seconds and green for the last minute, each from the average (dashed bottom edge) to the peak (solid top edge). To use a peak as the reference, as on the old levels page, set Levels › Ref from to 5 s peak or 60 s peak.

There are three ways to measure loudness. Switch with the LUFS ▸ button or M:

  • LUFS (short-term): the broadcast and streaming loudness standard (ITU-R BS.1770), averaged over 3 seconds. Streaming platforms target around −14 LUFS for finished programme; for a live feed what matters is staying consistent. This is the best match for how loud something sounds.
  • Leq 3 s: the SPL meter's weighting (A, C or Z) and calibration, averaged over 3 seconds. Use it with a calibrated mic to watch room level.
  • RMS 3 s: plain unweighted signal level in dBFS.
Coming from the old audiogeek pages

The old levels monitor's ceiling line is the reference here; its Use 5 Sec Peak and Use 1 Min Peak buttons are the 5 s peak and 60 s peak options under Ref from, and its red and green boxes appear at the 60 s view. The old analyzer's waterfall is the Bands tab, and bands you ignored there stay ignored.

The bright line is the 3-second level; the thin line behind it is the fast 400 ms (momentary) level that the peaks are taken from. Levels can watch either input: the measurement mic, or the reference input, for example a desk or stream send patched into your interface. Switch with the Mic in ▸ button or Levels › Source. Both are recorded all the time and each keeps its own reference, and the reference input is a line signal, so it reads in dBFS without mic calibration. Levels keeps recording while you look at other tabs, so you can switch away and come back to the whole history. The history is kept in memory only, so it starts again if you reload or close clever. Each method keeps its own reference.

Bands: which fader do I pull?

The Bands tab splits the sound into the same bands as a graphic EQ, one column per fader. Time scrolls upward with the newest moment at the bottom, and the wider a band's mark, the louder it is. The loudest band at each moment is drawn in red: the hot zone.

Choose a layout that matches your graphic with the button in the header or under Bands › Layout: 31-band (20 Hz–20 kHz, the most common), 33-band (from 12.5 Hz), 30-band (from 25 Hz), 15-band 2/3-octave, or 10-band octave. For anything else choose Custom and type the centre frequencies printed on your graphic's faceplate, such as 63 125 250 500 1k 2k 4k 8k.

The hot strip under the waterfall shows how often each band has been the loudest over the last 10 seconds, and the large readout at the top right names the band that's been hot most often. Music spreads the wins around; a band that's ringing wins nearly all the time, so when the readout turns red and shows a high percentage, that's the fader to pull. Knowing the exact frequency doesn't help much on an analog graphic; knowing the band does.

The silence gate stops Bands from naming the loudest part of the noise floor when nothing is happening. While the overall level is below the gate, nothing counts as hot, the readout stays hidden and the strip says below silence gate. The default is −60 dBFS; with the room quiet, press Bands › Set from noise to put the gate 6 dB above the current noise.

The Mic in / Ref in / Solo button picks what Bands looks at. Solo is the second reference channel (see the Voice check): with the desk's solo bus patched there, you can solo a wedge send or a mic group mid-show and see which band is hottest in it without touching the main measurement. Bands keeps about two minutes of history for every input, so switching source shows that input's recent past straight away. The solo history only shows whatever happened to be soloed at the time, so keep in mind what was soloed when you read back through it.

The Z / A / C button weights the bands. A-weighting turns the lows right down, roughly the way the ear hears them at moderate levels, so the hot band follows what's most audible rather than what has the most energy; C is nearly flat with a gentle roll-off at the extremes. The weighting is applied when drawing, so the whole history switches with it. The level, gate and readout are then in weighted dBFS.

Tap a band's label to ignore it (useful for air-conditioning rumble or a constant hum), and the hot zone and tally will skip it. Use all bands brings them all back. Ignored bands are remembered by frequency, so they stay ignored if you change layout. Set the display range under Bands › Floor / Ceiling. Like Levels, Bands can show the mic or the reference input (Mic in ▸), for example to see what the send through the graphic carries.

09Voice check: is this a clean vocal?

The Voice tab looks at a speech mic the way an experienced engineer listens to one. A voice should carry most of its energy between about 100 Hz and 5 kHz. Below 100 Hz is mostly pops, handling noise and rumble; above 5 kHz is texture and sibilance. If most of the energy is above 5 kHz, something is wrong.

While someone talks, clever builds an average spectrum of their voice. It only listens while the level is above the speech gate, so pauses don't count. It compares the result with the shape of natural speech, drawn as a grey band, and lists what it finds, with suggested channel-strip cuts:

  • Rumble and pops below 100 Hz: a high-pass frequency.
  • Low-mid build-up (150–450 Hz): usually a talker too close to a directional mic. A broad cut.
  • Resonances: narrow peaks, often from a lectern, a hard surface or the mic itself, and often where feedback starts. A narrow notch.
  • Nasal, harsh and sibilant regions: gentle cuts, or a de-esser for sibilance.
  • Thin or lacking presence: these get advice rather than an EQ, because boosting costs feedback margin. Moving the mic usually fixes them.

The bar at the top of the panel shows how the energy splits across the three zones. Each suggestion is numbered on the graph, and the dashed line shows the predicted result. Cutting is generally safe, unless it takes so much out of the speech band that you need more gain to hear the talker, which eats into feedback margin. So clever estimates how much speech level each cut costs and warns when the total gets too high.

The Voice check can listen to a third input, so you can leave the RTA and transfer function running and glance at it now and then. If your reference comes from a stereo input, tick Inputs › Other channel is a second input. The reference uses the channel chosen under Ref channel, the other channel becomes Solo with its own meter, and Voice listens to it. A desk's solo bus is a good choice: solo the vocal mic and Voice checks it without touching anything else. Voice keeps collecting in the background whichever tab is showing.

Loud, brief sounds such as a phone ringing or a burst of feedback dominate the average for a while. Press New talker to clear it, and also when someone else steps up. Set gate from noise, pressed while nobody is talking, puts the speech gate just above the room noise.

If the suggestions feel too eager for your system or your talkers, tune them in the Voice settings. Sensitivity raises or lowers every trigger at once: Gentle needs half as much deviation again before anything is flagged. Cut depth scales every suggested cut, so Half turns a −6 dB suggestion into −3 dB. Under Rules each check can be switched off or given its own frequency range, trigger, maximum cut and Q, and the resonance detector, the edges of the voice zone and the cost warning are there too. Changes apply straight away, so you can adjust them while the talker is still speaking. Changed values are outlined; Reset to defaults puts everything back.

10Transfer function: setting it up

The transfer function needs a reference signal. There are two ways to get one.

Option A: internal loopback (quickest)

clever generates pink noise itself, plays it out of the computer's audio output, and uses its own copy as the reference. Set Inputs › Reference to Generator (internal loopback). Connect the computer's output to the system you want to measure: a speaker, or a channel on the desk.

This measures everything from the computer's output to the mic, including the desk and its EQ, which is often exactly what you want.

Option B: external reference (how pros set it up)

Use an audio interface with at least two inputs. Send the signal (pink noise from clever, or music) into the desk. Patch a feed of the signal into interface input 1 at the point where your measurement should start, often the desk's output before the system processor. Connect the measurement mic to input 2. In clever, set Measurement to the interface, channel 2, and Reference to the interface, channel 1.

Now the transfer function shows only what happens after the reference point, so you can measure the processor, amps and speakers without the desk's channel EQ getting in the way. It also works with music during a show.

Option C: play your own music through clever

No desk handy? Plug a music source (a CD player, a phone, a laptop) into the reference input of your interface, set Reference to that device, and tick Pass the reference through to the output. clever plays the reference straight to the Output device, so you can feed the amp directly, and measures with the music as the reference. It replaces the generator, which is muted while pass-through is on.

  • For a stereo source, choose Ref channel › L+R (mono sum) so the reference matches what both speakers play. This needs the source on its own device.
  • If the source and the mic share one interface, only the reference channel is passed through, so the mic never reaches the amp. Never pass through a live mic.
  • The browser adds some output latency (shown under the checkbox). Find delay measures the whole round trip, from the captured reference through the browser, the amp and the air to the mic, so press it once the music is playing.
  • Music works as a reference, but quiet passages and sparse arrangements lower coherence. Busy, full-range music gives the steadiest traces.

Then, every time

  1. Turn the generator on with a low Level, around −30 dBFS, and bring it up gently until the mic's MEAS meter sits around −30 to −15 dBFS.
  2. Press Find delay. Sound takes time to travel from the speaker to the mic (about 2.9 ms per metre), and the computer adds its own delay. clever finds the total and lines the reference up with the measurement. Without this step the plots are mostly noise.
  3. Watch the phase and magnitude plots settle. Press Reset averages if you move the mic.
Look after ears and drivers

Pink noise is loud and fatiguing, and full-level noise can damage tweeters. Start low, wear hearing protection for long sessions, and warn people in the room before you turn it on.

11Reading the transfer function plots

Magnitude: louder or quieter at each frequency

The bottom plot. The line shows how much louder (above 0) or quieter (below 0) the system makes each frequency compared with what went in. A perfectly flat system would be a flat line. Hills are frequencies the system exaggerates; valleys are ones it loses.

The line's height depends on gain settings, so its absolute position isn't important; its shape is. Press Center trace to slide it to the middle of the plot.

Coherence: can you trust this part of the plot?

The red line in the strip along the top of the magnitude plot, from 0 to 1. It tells you how consistently the measurement relates to the reference at each frequency.

  • Near 1: solid, trustworthy data.
  • Low or jumping: something else is going on. Usually background noise, heavy reverberation, too little signal at that frequency, or the delay not being set.

You can change how coherence is drawn with Coh. view, by clicking Coherence above the plot, or with K:

  • Lane: coherence gets its own strip along the top, so it never covers the magnitude trace.
  • Overlay: a faint coherence line over the top part of the plot, with the live trace drawn on top.
  • Mist: no line; a red haze appears behind frequencies where coherence is poor.
  • Fade: no line; the live trace itself fades where coherence is poor.
  • Off: magnitude only.

With Coh. blanking turned on, clever hides the magnitude and phase wherever coherence falls below the threshold, so you only see data worth acting on.

Phase: is everything arriving at the same time?

The middle plot shows the timing of each frequency, in degrees from −180° to +180°. When the line reaches the top it wraps to the bottom, which is normal and doesn't mean anything has jumped.

  • Flat near 0°: everything arrives together, the ideal.
  • A steady slope that wraps more and more often towards high frequencies: the delay is set wrong. Press Find delay again.
  • A gentle bend in one region: normal around crossovers and the low end of a speaker's range.
  • Everything shifted by 180°: something is wired in reverse polarity. Invert measurement polarity lets you confirm.

Phase matters most where two sources overlap, such as subs and mains at the crossover. If their phase traces line up there, they add together; if they're far apart, they cancel.

Delay and the impulse view

The Impulse tab in the top plot shows the system's response to a single click. A tall spike at 0 ms means the delay is set correctly. If the spike sits off zero, press Add to delay to correct it. Smaller spikes after the main one are reflections from walls and ceilings arriving later.

Listening to the difference

The Listen section in the settings panel plays the inputs to a pair of headphones, on their own output if your browser supports choosing one. Choose what to hear:

  • Mic and Reference: each input on its own, for comparison.
  • Mic − reference: the reference subtracted after the measured delay and a matched level. Because the system changes the tone, this mostly sounds like a thin, phasey ghost of the programme: the difference in tone you can hear. Below about 100 Hz it can even add bass the mic didn't hear.
  • Mic − predicted: the reference is first passed through the measured impulse response, then subtracted. Everything the system does predictably cancels, leaving what the reference can't explain: room noise, distortion, the reverb tail beyond about a third of a second, and anything that changes over time. It is the sound of low coherence.

The section shows how much cancellation to expect, worked out from the coherence. In a simulated room, Mic − predicted removed about 20 dB; a real room will usually manage 10 to 20 dB, best in the lows. Press Find delay first, keep both inputs on one interface, and use headphones only: if the mic can hear this output, it will feed back.

Averages and smoothing

  • Averages: how many measurements are combined. More gives a steadier, more reliable trace but reacts more slowly. 8 to 16 is a good default; use 1 or 2 to see changes instantly.
  • Smoothing: blurs the trace across neighbouring frequencies. 1/24 octave shows real detail; 1/6 or 1/3 shows the overall tonal shape, closer to what you hear. Don't EQ fine wiggles you can only see with no smoothing.
  • FFT › Multi-time window: clever automatically uses long analysis windows for bass and short ones for treble, so both are sharp. Leave it on unless you have a reason not to.

12Traces: before and after

A trace is a frozen snapshot of the transfer function. Press Capture (or C) and the current magnitude and phase are stored and drawn in a fixed colour behind the live trace.

  • Capture before you EQ, then watch the live trace move as you adjust.
  • Capture at several seats to see what's the same everywhere (fix it with EQ) and what changes from seat to seat (it's the room, and EQ won't help).
  • Rename traces in the list so you remember what they were, and switch each one on or off with its tick box.
  • Export CSV saves traces as files you can keep or open in a spreadsheet; Import… loads them back, including frequency response files from other tools.

Traces are kept in this browser between sessions. Export the ones that matter, because clearing browser data deletes them.

13Common jobs, step by step

Tune a PA with EQ

  1. Set up the transfer function (section 10) with the mic at a typical listening position, about ear height, away from walls.
  2. Find delay, use 1/6 octave smoothing, 16 averages, and coherence blanking at 0.5. Press Center trace.
  3. Capture the trace. Move the mic to two or three other seats and capture there too.
  4. Look for broad shapes that appear at every position with good coherence. Those are the system; EQ them with wide, gentle filters.
  5. Watch the live trace while you EQ, and compare with your "before" capture. Leave narrow dips alone.
  6. Finish by listening to familiar music. The measurement guides you; your ears make the final call.

Line up subs with the main speakers

  1. Mute the subs, measure the mains, find delay, and capture as "Mains".
  2. Mute the mains, unmute the subs, and capture as "Subs" without pressing Find delay again, so both traces share the same timing.
  3. On the phase plot, look at the crossover region where the two magnitude traces are at similar levels. Adjust the delay on whichever arrives first (in the processor, not in clever) until the phase traces overlap there.
  4. Unmute both. The combined magnitude should now be fuller through the crossover rather than showing a dip.

Ring out monitors or catch feedback

  1. Use the Spectrograph view, or the RTA at 1/12 or 1/24 octave with peak hold on.
  2. Slowly raise the monitor gain with the mic in position. When a single frequency starts to ring, it appears as a growing vertical stripe or a spike.
  3. Hover or tap on it for the exact frequency, cut it with a narrow EQ filter, and continue.

Ring out a room or monitors with an analog graphic

  1. Open the Bands tab and set the layout to match your graphic, so each column is one fader.
  2. Feed clever from where it can hear the ring: a measurement mic near the wedge or in the room, or the send that goes through the graphic, through an audio interface.
  3. With the room quiet, press Set from noise so the readout ignores the noise floor.
  4. Start with the graphic flat. With the performance mic open in position, raise the gain slowly until it starts to ring.
  5. Watch the large readout. When it turns red with a high percentage, pull that fader 3 to 6 dB, then back the gain off a touch.
  6. Bring the gain up again; the next band will take over. Stop after three or four cuts, or sooner if the mic starts to sound thin.
  7. If a constant noise keeps winning (air conditioning, a hum), tap its label to ignore it.

Clean up a lectern or panel mic

  1. Patch the vocal group's output, after its EQ, to the left channel of the reference input, and the desk's solo bus to the right. Set Ref channel to Channel 1 and tick Other channel is a second input. The measurement mic stays as it is, so the RTA and transfer function keep working.
  2. Solo the lectern mic. Open the Voice tab with the source on Solo. With nobody talking, press Set gate from noise.
  3. Have the talker speak normally for a few seconds; ten or more is better. Watch the energy split and the suggestions build up.
  4. Make the first suggestion on the channel strip, usually the high-pass or the biggest cut. The dashed line shows what to expect. Press New talker to measure again with the change in place.
  5. Stop when the list says the voice looks clean, or when the cost warning appears. After that, moving the mic or coaching the talker does more than EQ.

Keep a set under a noise limit

  1. Calibrate the mic with a calibrator (section 4), then place it where the limit applies, usually front of house.
  2. Set the weighting the limit specifies (usually A) and press Reset Leq / Max as the set starts.
  3. Keep the Leq figure under the limit. The live number can go over briefly; the average is what's usually enforced.

14Phone, keyboard and Stream Deck

On a phone or tablet

  • In Chrome's menu, choose Add to home screen. clever then opens full screen, with Android's status and navigation bars hidden (swipe in from an edge to bring them back for a moment). It works without a signal once it's been opened online at least once.
  • The screen stays on while clever is running.
  • Tap a plot to read the frequency and level at that point.
  • USB-C measurement mics such as the iMM-6C plug straight in.

Making room for the plots

  • The ☰ button (or S) hides the settings panel once you've set things up; press it again to bring it back. On a phone it slides the panel in and out.
  • The ⛶ button (or F) switches the browser to full screen.
  • The ALL PANES button (or L) switches between all three plots and the top plot on its own.

Stream Deck

On a computer with Chrome or Edge, an Elgato Stream Deck (XL, MK.2 or V2) can run clever hands-free. Quit the Elgato software first, then press Connect Stream Deck. The keys show live values: generator, delay, smoothing, averages, trace on/off, SPL, Leq and input levels. Tap the Generator key to change between pink, white and tone; hold it for half a second to switch the generator on or off. The Layout key next to it switches between the top plot on its own and all panes. At the end of the third row, New talker restarts the Voice check. It also has a level meter for the input Voice listens to (the solo channel, if you use one), a dot while speech is above the gate, and the seconds of speech collected, and Voice summarises the check: green when clean, amber or red with the number of suggested cuts. Pressing it opens the Voice tab with its list, or hides the list. The deck has toggles for the first four traces. Tick Show on-screen console to get the same buttons on screen, which also works on phones; tap and hold work there too.

17Glossary

dB (decibel)
A ratio on a logarithmic scale. +6 dB is about double the voltage; +10 dB sounds roughly twice as loud.
dBFS
Level relative to the loudest signal the input can record. 0 dBFS is the ceiling, so normal readings are negative.
dB SPL
Sound pressure level in the air. 94 dB SPL is the standard calibration level; a loud concert is often 100 dB or more.
Octave
A doubling of frequency, such as 100 to 200 Hz. A 1/3 octave band is a third of that span.
Pink noise
Random noise with equal energy per octave. It sounds like a waterfall and looks flat on an RTA, which makes it the standard test signal.
FFT
Fast Fourier transform, the maths that splits a signal into its frequencies.
Transfer function
What a system does to a signal passing through it, found by comparing its output with its input.
Coherence
A 0 to 1 measure of how trustworthy the transfer function is at each frequency.
Phase
Timing at each frequency, in degrees. 360° is one full cycle at that frequency.
Polarity
Whether a signal is right way up or inverted. Reversed polarity shows as a 180° phase shift at every frequency.
Impulse response
How a system responds to a single click. Its peak shows the arrival time; later peaks are reflections.
LUFS
Loudness units relative to full scale: the broadcast loudness measure from ITU-R BS.1770, weighted to match how loud things sound. A 1 dB change in LUFS is a 1 dB change in level.
Leq
Equivalent continuous level: the steady level with the same total energy as a fluctuating one over a period.
A / C weighting
Filters on the SPL meter. A follows the ear at moderate levels; C keeps most of the bass.
Crossover
The frequency range where one speaker (say, the sub) hands over to another (the main box).
FOH
Front of house: the mixing position in the audience area, and the system that covers the audience.
